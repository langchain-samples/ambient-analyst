### GLP-1 Drugs Reshaping Food, Retail, and Healthcare Economics (verified July 2026)

### GLP-1 Drugs Reshaping Food, Retail, and Healthcare Economics (verified July 2026)
- GLP-1 adoption is now large enough to matter for consumer baskets and payer budgets. Reuters reported in 2026 that about **15% of U.S. households** used GLP-1s in a Circana-linked snapshot, while Forbes/industry summaries cite about **10 million U.S. users in 2025** and a potential **25 million by 2030**. Primary implication: the demand shock is no longer hypothetical; it is already changing category mix and coverage decisions.
- Food/retail impact is mix shift, not total collapse. Reuters (Feb. 8, 2026) reported GLP-1 users consume roughly **40% fewer calories**, translating to about **4%–6% smaller grocery baskets** and **4%–5% less spending on fast food**. Reuters (Mar. 10, 2026) also reported Lindt data showing U.S. premium chocolate sales among GLP-1 users rose **~17% in 2025** versus **6.5%** among non-users, suggesting shoppers buy less volume but sometimes trade up to higher-quality/protein-dense items.
- Retail response is shifting to smaller portions, higher protein, and “better-for-you” rebranding. Reuters reported in Feb. 2026 that companies from PepsiCo to Coca-Cola were emphasizing shorter ingredient lists and smaller pack sizes, while food capex was rising as firms reworked portfolios. The strategic read: GLP-1s are forcing CPG brands to optimize for satiety, protein density, and premiumization rather than mass snacking.
- Healthcare economics are being pulled in both directions: near-term pharmacy spend rises, long-term comorbidity costs may fall. KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey says the share of large firms covering GLP-1s for weight loss rose sharply in 2025, but many employers also reported higher-than-expected use and prescription-cost pressure. KFF Medicaid analysis shows GLP-1 prescriptions in Medicaid increased from about **1 million (2019)** to **over 8 million (2024)** and gross spending from roughly **$1B** to **almost $9B**. The current federal response is to lower access friction, not eliminate cost pressure: CMS announced a **$50/month** Medicare GLP-1 bridge beginning **July 1, 2026** and a BALANCE model to broaden access over time.
- Counter-case: the category impact may be slower and more uneven than headlines imply. Some GLP-1 users still buy treats, and the Lindt/Reuters data suggest not every “bad” category loses volume immediately. Oral pills could broaden adoption, but if coverage tightens, adherence falls, or prices remain restrictive, the macro effect will be a gradual mix shift rather than a sudden demand cliff.
- Decision implication: investors/operators should assume **persistent category bifurcation**—losers are impulse snacks, sugary beverages, and some fast food baskets; winners are high-protein, portion-controlled, premium indulgence, and health-oriented offerings. In healthcare, GLP-1s are likely to remain a major employer/payer cost item in the near term even if they reduce downstream complications over a multi-year horizon.
- Underpriced second-order effects to watch: (1) **benefits design churn** as more employers and insurers back away from blanket coverage or move to strict utilization management; (2) **obesity-drug logistics and pharmacy operations** as oral pills, compounding restrictions, and cold-chain/dispensing rules change fulfillment; (3) **product-mix inflation** in food/retail, where unit volumes fall but protein/premium items can preserve or raise dollars; (4) **spillovers into sleep apnea, cardiometabolic care, and other downstream specialties** as coverage expands to new indications and utilization patterns shift.

### Japan Equities — Structural Bull Case (verified August 2026)
- Japan’s bull case is structural, not just cyclical: corporate governance reform, better capital management, an inflationary rather than deflationary macro regime, and a still-modest valuation backdrop are combining to change the market’s earnings and payout profile.
- Tokyo Stock Exchange reforms are a central driver. JPX says the TSE issues the "TSE Listed Companies White Paper on Corporate Governance 2025" and has built a system to track and publicize listed-company governance progress. Source: https://www.jpx.co.jp/english/equities/listing/cg/02.html
- Bank of America Global Research said in 2025 that three key forces are driving Japan’s market revival: corporate reforms; increased international and domestic allocations to Japanese equities; and a macro environment that has normalized, moving from deflation to inflation. It also said the Nikkei 225 has risen by over 40% since April 2025 and that Japan now has one of the best performing stock markets in the world. Source: https://business.bofa.com/jp/en/japan-insights/market-equities-growth-trend.html
- The macro backdrop matters because deflation is the enemy of nominal earnings growth and pricing power. Vanguard’s 2026 Japan outlook says the Bank of Japan is becoming more confident that the economy can withstand further policy normalization, with upside inflation risks supporting continued rate hikes, and that the global AI investment cycle is providing an additional tailwind to external demand and business investment. Source: https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/corporatesite/us/en/corp/vemo/vemo-japan.html
- BOJ data also point to a more normal nominal environment. In the Bank of Japan’s April 2026 outlook, the economy is expected to keep growing moderately and CPI ex-fresh-food is projected to be in the range of 2.5-3.0 percent in fiscal 2026, with moves to pass on wage increases to selling prices continuing. Source: https://www.boj.or.jp/en/mopo/outlook/gor2604b.pdf
- That matters for equities because Japan’s long-standing problem was not lack of profits alone; it was weak reinvestment discipline, low returns on equity, and cash hoarding. Governance pressure and market discipline are pushing firms toward buybacks, higher dividends, and divestitures of idle assets, which can lift per-share earnings even if top-line growth is only moderate.
- The valuation argument is powerful but incomplete: Japan still screens cheaper than the U.S. on many measures, but cheapness only matters if capital allocation improves. The real bull case is that governance reform can help close that gap over time by converting balance-sheet efficiency into shareholder returns.
- Counter-case: the rally can stall if yen weakness reverses sharply, if energy-import costs pressure margins, if wage growth fails to translate into durable domestic demand, or if reforms remain box-ticking rather than behavior-changing. Deloitte notes that a weaker yen and higher energy costs can dampen inflation relief and slow wage-driven growth. Source: https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/economy/asia-pacific/japan-economic-outlook.html
- Decision implication: the best Japan exposure is not a blanket macro bet; it is a bet on firms with excess cash, low valuations, credible governance change, and exposure to domestic nominal growth or global demand. The structural winner set should include banks, insurers, industrials, exporters with pricing power, and firms actively returning capital.
- Supply underinvestment matters most when it combines with long lead times, declining ore grades, and concentrated bottlenecks in refining/smelting: it doesn’t guarantee a broad supercycle, but it makes the market more likely to reprice deficits upward and stay volatile for longer.

### Natural Gas Demand / Power Mix (verified July 2026)
- Gas is **not** going away over the 2026-2030 window. The IEA’s **Gas 2025** base case says global gas demand grows by around **9% by 2030**, or about **380 bcm**, at an average rate of nearly **1.5%** per year from 2024 to 2030. The **power sector** is the second-largest contributor to global gas demand growth, accounting for **over a third** of the net increase, while Asia Pacific provides more than half of power-sector demand growth. Sources: https://www.iea.org/reports/gas-2025/executive-summary ; https://www.iea.org/news/coming-surge-in-lng-production-is-set-to-reshape-global-gas-markets.
- The IEA’s **Electricity 2026** outlook says renewables, natural gas, and nuclear together are expected to meet all additional global electricity demand through 2030, and that gas-fired generation grows by an average of **2.6% per year** through 2030, driven mainly by rising U.S. electricity demand and fuel switching from oil to gas in the Middle East. Source: https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/executive-summary ; https://www.iea.org/news/global-electricity-demand-is-set-to-grow-strongly-to-2030-underscoring-need-for-investments-in-grids-and-flexibility.
- In the U.S., EIA’s January 2026 STEO said natural gas generation is forecast to be **flat in 2026** and then rise by **1% in 2027**, while EIA’s later 2026 STEO updates show U.S. natural gas-fired generation totaling **1,696 BkWh in 2026** and **1,711 BkWh in 2027**, with gas’s share of total power generation falling to **39% in 2027** versus **40% in 2025**. Source: https://www.eia.gov/pressroom/releases/press582.php ; https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67005 ; Reuters coverage https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/us-power-use-beat-record-highs-2026-2027-ai-use-surges-eia-says-2026-07-07/.
- Counter-case: if solar, storage, transmission, and grid interconnection continue to scale faster than expected, gas could be squeezed harder after 2027 and see more of its growth come from LNG exports, industry, and Middle East power switching than from OECD power markets.
- Practical implication: the next few years look less like a gas-demand collapse and more like a **bifurcation** — flat-to-modest growth in OECD power demand, continued structural LNG/export support, and stronger growth in Asia and the Middle East. Gas remains the balancing fuel for renewables, but its share of the generation mix should erode gradually rather than abruptly.

### Solar/Storage/Gas Value-Chain Margin Durability (verified July 2026)
- **Most durable margins in solar + storage are at the grid bottlenecks and balance-of-system chokepoints**, not in commodity-like modules. IEA says power transformer and cable prices/procurement times have almost doubled in four years, and more than **1,600 GW** of solar and wind projects are waiting for grid connections; power transmission investment would need to exceed **$200 billion annually by the mid-2030s** to keep up. Source: https://www.iea.org/news/rising-component-prices-and-supply-chain-pressures-are-hindering-the-development-of-transmission-grid-infrastructure.
- **Power equipment / electrification suppliers** currently show the clearest pricing power. Reuters reported GE Vernova lifted its 2026 adjusted core profit margin forecast to **12% to 14%** and Siemens Energy raised its 2026 profit-margin outlook to **10% to 12%** on surging demand for data-centre-related power equipment and grid components. Source: https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/ge-vernova-lifts-annual-revenue-forecast-data-center-demand-2026-04-22/ ; https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/siemens-energy-raises-outlook-2026-04-23/.
- **Battery storage developers and integrators** can earn durable returns where they control interconnection, site, and dispatch value, but the business is still constrained by grid access and component supply. Reuters reported battery-storage firms are eyeing AI/data-centre demand but face grid and supply hurdles; batteries are becoming essential where gas generation cannot ramp fast enough. Source: https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/battery-storage-firms-eye-ai-demand-face-grid-supply-hurdles-2026-05-18/.
- **Project developers / EPCs** are generally lower-margin than equipment bottlenecks because returns are more exposed to execution risk, financing, and competitive bidding. The durable edge is in permitting, land, interconnection rights, and standardized replication at scale, not in bespoke construction.
- **Upstream gas and LNG infrastructure** have durable economics where capacity is contracted and the asset is hard to replace. Reuters reported Cheniere raised its 2026 adjusted core profit forecast to **$7.25 billion to $7.75 billion**, with margins on open 2027 LNG capacity around **$6 to $7 per mmBtu**, and Venture Global raised its 2026 fixed liquefaction fee assumptions to **$9.50 to $10.50 per mmBtu**. Source: https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/cheniere-energy-posts-first-quarter-loss-2026-05-07/ ; https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/venture-global-raises-annual-core-profit-forecast-higher-liquefaction-fees-lng-2026-05-12/.
- **Most exposed to margin compression:** commodity solar modules, generic battery cells, and merchant power producers without contracted offtake. Those are closer to pass-through businesses once supply catches up.
- Decision implication: the highest-quality economics are in **grid bottlenecks, power equipment, interconnection, and contracted infrastructure**; the weakest are in commoditized hardware and merchant generation. In gas, the best margins sit in **LNG liquefaction/export and contracted infrastructure**, not in undifferentiated upstream production.

### AI and Enterprise Software Unit Economics (verified July 2026)
- AI is changing enterprise software economics in two opposite directions at once: it can **expand revenue per customer** via premium AI features, usage-based billing, and higher attach rates, but it can also **compress gross margin** because inference costs, forward-deployed implementation, and model-routing complexity add variable cost.
- Reuters (Feb. 23, 2026) reported OpenAI's enterprise push relies on consulting partners and forward-deployed engineers to move customers beyond pilots; this is a structural sign that AI monetization increasingly includes service/implementation layers, not just seat licenses. Reuters (Jun. 3, 2026) also reported investors favor vendors moving to usage-based pricing and selecting AI-friendly pricing models over pure headcount subscriptions.
- ServiceNow (Jan. 28, 2026) said its AI partnerships and acquisitions supported higher annual subscription revenue guidance, illustrating the upside side of the equation: AI can lift subscription growth when it is embedded into core workflows.
- Counter-case: AI does not automatically improve software economics. Reuters (May 27, 2026) reported Salesforce forecast revenue below estimates amid fears that AI rivals could disrupt traditional software demand; AI can shift value from seat-based SaaS to workflows, services, or infrastructure, and some vendors may lose pricing power if customers route around their old products.
- Practical implication: the winners are likely to be vendors that (1) own workflow data and distribution, (2) can bill by outcome or usage, and (3) can keep inference costs below the incremental value captured. The losers are vendors stuck in flat per-seat pricing with weak workflow lock-in.

### Hyperscaler AI Capex — Sustainability vs Digestion Phase (verified July 2026)
- The debate is now about **pace and financing quality**, not whether AI capex exists. Reuters (July 22, 2026) said Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle are expected to spend more combined on capex than they generate in free cash flow by **2027**, and that current-year capex estimates for those five had risen from about **$485 billion** in January to around **$730 billion** in July. Reuters also said the companies will generate about **$340 billion** more in annual operating cash flow in **2027** than in **2025**, but capex is expected to rise by roughly **$534 billion**, or about **$1.57 of additional investment for every $1 of additional cash flow**.
- There is still a credible sustainability case. Reuters noted Microsoft said its AI business has surpassed a **$37 billion annual revenue run rate**, while Amazon reported **28%** growth at AWS in its first quarter. Alphabet’s Q2 2026 revenue was **$119.8 billion**, up **24%**, and Google Cloud revenue rose **82%** to **$24.8 billion**; Alphabet also said its capex grew **100% year-over-year** to **$44.9 billion** and raised its 2026 capex guide to **$195 billion-$205 billion**. Those numbers argue that demand is real and monetization is improving, at least for the leaders. Sources: Reuters July 22, 2026; CNBC July 22, 2026.
- But the digestion-phase case is getting stronger. Reuters’ July 22 analysis says the capex estimates cover all spending, not just AI-related spending, and that the buildout is increasingly squeezing free cash flow. Reuters cited Oracle as the clearest stress case: capex rose from **47%** of operating cash flow in fiscal 2022 to **174%** in fiscal 2026, with capex at **$55.7 billion** against **$32 billion** of operating cash flow and free cash flow turning negative. Reuters also reported that the five hyperscalers are starting to show returns, but investors are focused on whether revenue growth can keep pace with the spending surge.
- The strongest counter-argument to a near-term digestion phase is strategic self-defense: no hyperscaler wants to underbuild while competitors are racing ahead, and much of the spend is on durable assets—data centers, power, networking, chips, and land. A second counter-case is that a meaningful share of the apparent capex surge is inflation, not just volume. A July 19, 2026 commentary cited estimates that **20% to 30%** of the next increase in AI capex may reflect inflation, and another piece said soaring memory prices could explain about **45%** of capex growth at big cloud companies this year. That means nominal capex can overstate real capacity added.
- My read: **leaders can sustain elevated capex for longer than bears expect, but the marginal dollar is moving into a digestion phase**. The first phase was funded mainly by operating cash flow; the next phase increasingly depends on debt, equity, vendor financing, or slower monetization payback. Alphabet’s **$84.75 billion** equity raise in June and commentary that Oracle may need about **$40 billion** of combined debt and equity for fiscal 2027 show how financing is broadening. That is not a liquidity crisis, but it is a clear shift from “self-funding buildout” toward “capital-intensive race.”
- Practical implication: expect **continued capex upcycle, but weaker free-cash-flow optics, more capital-market dependence, and higher scrutiny of ROI**. The market is likely to reward evidence of utilization, backlog conversion, and cloud AI revenue inflection, and punish capex that outruns monetization. The digestion phase, if it arrives, is more likely to be an **earnings/multiple digestion** than a sharp spending collapse.

### Climate Risk and Insurance Economics (verified August 2026)
- Climate risk is reshaping insurance economics through four channels: **higher expected losses, capital/reinsurance repricing, underwriting pullback in the most exposed areas, and more spending on data/modeling/resilience**.
- Swiss Re’s 2025 natcat reporting says global economic losses from natural catastrophes were **USD220bn in 2025**, about **49%** of which were insured, and that global insured natural catastrophe losses were **USD107bn** in 2025; wildfires, severe convective storms and floods alone accounted for **88%** of total global insured natural catastrophe losses in 2025. Swiss Re also said its modelled peak-loss scenario for 2026 could climb to about **USD320bn** and that the upward trend in insured losses is structural. Sources: https://www.swissre.com/press-release/Wildfires-storms-floods-contribute-to-record-92-of-global-insured-losses-in-2025-says-Swiss-Re-Institute/7b39b1a5-b878-4a55-a5ff-bf5aa561a675 ; https://www.swissre.com/institute/research/sigma-research/sigma-2026-01-natcat-2025-wildfire-storm-risk/global-natcat-losses-2025.html
- Munich Re’s 2025 and 2026 updates point in the same direction: 2025 insured losses exceeded **US$100bn**, with a structural shift toward smaller but frequent events; in its 2026 first-half review Munich Re said global losses remained slightly below the ten-year average but that rising heat, growing exposure, changing hazards and reconstruction costs keep the long-term risk trend intact. Munich Re also said only a fraction of African catastrophe losses are insured, highlighting a persistent protection gap. Sources: https://www.munichre.com/en/company/media-relations/media-information-and-corporate-news/media-information/2026/natural-disaster-figures-2025.html ; https://www.munichre.com/en/company/media-relations/media-information-and-corporate-news/media-information/2026/natural-disaster-figures-first-half-2026.html
- The market-level implication is that insurers can no longer rely on historical frequency/severity patterns. That forces **higher catastrophe loads, tighter underwriting, more exclusions or deductibles in high-risk zones, and higher reinsurance costs**, especially for property/casualty lines. NAIC materials show U.S. regulators are actively updating catastrophe risk factors, climate disclosures, and impact analysis, while IAIS says climate-related risks remain a high-priority supervisory area and specifically flags data gaps and uncertainty around tail risks. Sources: https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/publication-syn-zs-26-01.pdf ; https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/risk-based-capital ; https://www.iais.org/uploads/2026/07/Global-Insurance-Market-Report-2026-mid-year-update.pdf
- That has two economic effects. First, **premiums rise faster than GDP in exposed geographies**, but not always enough to offset loss cost inflation, so underwriting margins can still deteriorate. Second, **availability becomes as important as price**: some homes, businesses, and coastal/wildfire risks become harder or more expensive to insure, which pushes business to state-backed pools, residual markets, or self-insurance. Ceres’ 2026 report says disclosure quality is uneven across **537 insurance groups**, and argues that robust climate data is needed to price risk and align capital with exposure. Source: https://www.ceres.org/resources/reports/the-2026-progress-report-climate-risk-reporting-in-the-insurance-sector
- Reinsurance and capital are being repriced upward, but not uniformly. Swiss Re said continued underwriting discipline remains vital in a more competitive market, and Munich Re’s RiskScan 2026 says insurers are increasingly expected to contribute to **risk prevention, mitigation, and resilience-building**, not just post-event claims handling. That implies the insurance margin pool is shifting from pure risk transfer toward **data, modeling, engineering, loss prevention, and resilience services**.
- Counter-case: insurance is not a simple “climate losses up, profits down” story. Better pricing, higher investment income, improved modeling, and the ability to retreat from unprofitable zones can preserve ROE for stronger carriers and reinsurers. Some regions and perils also saw below-trend losses in H1 2026, and the industry can pass through part of the loss inflation via rate increases and tighter terms. Sources: Swiss Re and Munich Re 2026 updates above.
- Decision implication: climate risk is turning insurance into a **more selective, more capital-intensive, more data-driven business**. Winners are likely to be carriers/reinsurers with strong catastrophe modeling, disciplined underwriting, diversified books, and pricing power; losers are thinly capitalized players in the most exposed property lines and regions. For investors, the key question is not whether climate raises claims — it does — but who can **reprice fast enough, retreat where needed, and monetize resilience services** without destroying growth.

### Knock-on Effects for Real Estate and Mortgages (verified August 2026)
- Climate risk transmits from insurance into housing through **affordability, insurability, collateral values, and mortgage credit**. FHFA says climate risk is already increasing the cost and limiting the availability of insurance in some areas, making it harder to develop new housing supply in the most disaster-exposed places. Source: https://www.fhfa.gov/blog/statistics/the-need-to-address-climate-risk
- The first-order effect is a **higher all-in monthly housing payment**. A 2026 housing-insurance market report cited in this session says the average premium for a new policy rose **8.5% year over year**, and that premiums now represent roughly **9%** of the typical homeowner’s monthly mortgage payment; the same report said coverage availability improved nationally but high-risk states such as California, Florida, and Texas still rely heavily on E&S markets. Source: https://finance.yahoo.com/news/2026-home-insurance-predictions-turning-155400785.html
- That means the housing affordability constraint is no longer just the mortgage rate. It is increasingly **mortgage + insurance + taxes + maintenance**. FHFA notes that where insurance coverage remains available, cost pressure can make monthly ownership unaffordable and can also deter new supply in vulnerable regions. Source: https://www.fhfa.gov/blog/statistics/the-need-to-address-climate-risk
- For mortgages, climate risk affects **underwriting and secondary-market pricing**. FHFA research cited in this session says climate risk is not adequately priced into home prices, mortgage insurance rates, or guarantee fees, and argues that risk-based pricing would immediately influence developers, lenders, investors, and homeowners by reducing the likelihood of building and buying in flood-, wildfire-, and coastal-risk areas. Source: https://www.fhfa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-12/ClimateandHousingReport%E2%80%93Dr-Lindsay-Owens.pdf
- A practical lending channel is that **insurance non-renewals signal hazard risk** and can change borrower behavior. FHFA’s climate-risk summit noted research showing that wildfire insurance non-renewals in California encourage residents to search for homes with lower fire hazard, and that wildfire risk can raise interest rates at origination after a wildfire. Source: https://www.fhfa.gov/blog/insights/fhfas-fall-2024-econ-summit-on-climate-risk
- Another lending channel is **collateral quality and foreclosure/prepayment risk**. FHFA’s summit material cites research finding that private-label residential MBS with relatively high spatial correlation have higher likelihood of foreclosure and prepayment, and can face higher interest rates after a wildfire. That means climate exposure can move from property-level risk into security pricing. Source: https://www.fhfa.gov/blog/insights/fhfas-fall-2024-econ-summit-on-climate-risk
- Flood insurance is especially important for mortgage underwriting because federally backed loans in FEMA flood zones require flood coverage; if insurance gets more expensive or unavailable, some deals simply do not close or become harder to refinance. Source: the flood-insurance material retrieved this session and FHFA climate-risk material.
- The knock-on effect is likely **housing-market bifurcation**:
  - safer, lower-risk markets retain financing access and may attract demand
  - high-risk ZIP codes face lower buyer demand, more cash purchases, larger down payments, or lender pullback
  - insurance shortfalls can depress resale values and slow turnover
  - builders may redirect capital away from exposed geographies
- Regional banks and nonbank lenders also feel it. When insurance costs surge or coverage becomes unreliable, delinquency risk rises and loan servicing becomes messier because borrowers can become payment-stressed or fail escrow tests even before mortgage rates move.
- Counter-case: higher insurance and mortgage pricing can **force better risk pricing**, which may improve long-run resilience and reduce future losses. It can also support adaptation investments, mitigation upgrades, and better land-use decisions. But in the near term, it is clearly a drag on affordability and transaction volume.
- Decision implication: climate risk is likely to **slow housing activity in the riskiest markets, widen affordability gaps, and increase credit differentiation by geography**. Lenders, investors, and builders will increasingly need property-level hazard data, not just credit scores and LTVs. The biggest losers are marginal coastal, floodplain, and wildfire-exposed assets; the biggest winners are lenders and owners with better risk-adjusted collateral and stronger mitigation.

### Emerging-Market Local-Currency Debt (verified July 2026)
- Local-currency EM debt currently screens as a **selective entry point rather than a blanket buy**. IMF April 2026 says EM policy resilience has improved thanks to independent central banks, inflation-targeting regimes, stronger fiscal frameworks, and deeper local-currency bond markets that have reduced reliance on foreign-currency borrowing and expanded the domestic investor base.
- JPMorgan AM’s Q2 2026 outlook says EM local-currency debt has benefited from **elevated real yields, some USD weakness, carry, and modest duration gains**, while Q2 fund flows remained positive. That is supportive for returns if the dollar stays soft and inflation remains contained.
- Counter-case: the asset class is still exposed to **US inflation/rate surprises, geopolitics, trade/tariff shocks, and country-specific fiscal/political risks**; IMF warns weaker EMs still face sovereign-bank nexus risks, especially where local sovereign debt is a larger share of bank assets.
- Practical implication: the best risk/reward is likely in countries with **credible disinflation, positive real rates, stable external balances, and deep domestic buyer bases**. Avoid treating the whole EM local curve as one trade.
- As of July 2026, the cleaner relative-value screens appear to be **Mexico, Brazil, and Indonesia**, while **India** looks fundamentally strong but often richer on valuation; **South Africa** offers carry but comes with more political/fiscal noise and higher volatility.

NATO and European governments are materially raising spending on drones, counter-drone, electronic warfare, sensors, and secure communications, reflecting a structural shift from platforms to autonomous networks. This is one of the clearest non-cyclical tech spend themes. Source set: CNBC July 2026; NATO / UK defence announcements; Reuters / defense coverage.

+- Rising defense spending flows first into **munitions, rocket motors, propellants, guidance electronics, radar, secure comms, drones, counter-drone, electronic warfare, satellites, and shipbuilding**, but the constraint is often **industrial throughput** rather than raw capital. Reuters reported on July 1, 2026 that startups are repurposing **automotive chips**, **fracking pipes**, and **pharmaceutical mixing techniques** to speed weapons output, while the Pentagon is setting aside **$53 billion** and simplifying procurement rules to expand critical missile and rocket production. Reuters reported July 15, 2026 that the U.S. has used long-term procurement contracts to give firms confidence to expand factories, with roughly **$20 billion** in private investment tied to plans to boost production of Patriot missiles and other high-demand weapons. Northrop and L3Harris are pushing R&D in **3D printing** and new mixing technologies, while missile shortages have been biting production. Source: Reuters July 15, 2026, https://www.reuters.com/business/aerospace-defense/trump-press-defense-executives-boost-weapons-production-2026-07-15/.

Reuters (July 21, 2026) reported Northrop’s 2026 sales rose to **$10.88 billion** for the quarter and backlog to **$104.7 billion** amid strong weapons demand; Reuters (July 22, 2026) said Europe’s defense spending is being paired with aerospace supply-chain investment, including a **£600 million** UK aerospace support package and new supply-chain funding. Source: Reuters July 21–22, 2026, https://www.reuters.com/business/aerospace-defense/northrop-grumman-raises-2026-forecasts-on-strong-weapons-demand-2026-07-21/ and https://www.reuters.com/business/aerospace-defense/us-politicians-pitch-aviation-space-business-farnborough-airshow-2026-07-22/.
+- The hardest bottlenecks are **solid rocket motors, curing ovens, X-ray inspection, rare earth magnets, and batteries**. The winners are firms with certified manufacturing capacity, long procurement visibility, and components that are dual-use or bottlenecked; the losers are commodity-like vendors that cannot pass certification, scale, or security requirements. Also, procurement is lumpy and political, so a lot of “spend” becomes backlog before it becomes revenue.
+- **Practical takeaway:** defense upcycles favor a narrow set of supply-chain winners: munitions, rocket motors, energetics, sensors, EO/IR, secure radios, drones/counter-drone, electronic warfare, shipyards, aerospace structures, specialty machining, and critical-mineral processing. For tech investors/operators, the watchlist is less “software broadly” and more **embedded systems, chips, secure comms, autonomy stacks, battery/propulsion components, and industrial automation that shortens production cycles**.

### July 2026 — Regional Bank Balance Sheet Watchlist
- **CRE concentration** is the first-order item: watch office and multifamily exposure, plus how much of the book is near maturity. The market risk is not just headline NPLs; it is whether banks need to refinance, extend, or modify loans into weaker collateral values and higher financing costs. Source set: OCC Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective; FDIC Q1 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile; verified 2026-07-20.
- **Funding mix / deposit stability** matters next. FDIC Q1 2026 shows deposits at FDIC-insured institutions rose to **$2.335T**, while **brokered deposits were $112.7B** and **FHLB advances were $90.6B**. Those are not crisis levels, but rising reliance on wholesale funding, brokered deposits, or expensive time deposits would signal margin pressure and lower liquidity cushion. Source: FDIC Q1 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile; verified 2026-07-20.
- **Unrealized securities losses** remain a balance-sheet fragility marker. FDIC Q1 2026 says unrealized losses remained elevated, while the Fed’s June 2026 supervision report says unrealized losses on securities portfolios fell and are at their lowest levels since 2021. If rates back up again, AFS/HTM marks can worsen and restrict optionality even if accounting losses are not immediately realized. Sources: FDIC Q1 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile; Federal Reserve Supervision and Regulation Report (June 2026).
- **Asset quality migration** is the next thing to watch after CRE: the FDIC says asset quality metrics remained generally favorable in Q1 2026 despite continued weakness in certain portfolios; the OCC says past-due and nonaccrual loans and net charge-offs remain below long-term averages in most portfolios, but refinancing risk in certain CRE segments and private credit markets warrants ongoing monitoring. Rising early-stage delinquencies in C&I, consumer, and CRE-adjacent portfolios would be an early warning before charge-offs accelerate. Sources: FDIC Q1 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile; OCC Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective.
- **Interest-rate sensitivity / NIM compression** is the profitability squeeze test. OCC’s Spring 2026 risk perspective says bank earnings improved and funding costs declined, but it still highlights elevated credit, market, operational, and compliance risks. For regionals, the key question is whether deposit costs keep falling faster than asset yields reset. Source: OCC Spring 2026 Semiannual Risk Perspective; verified 2026-07-20.
- **Private-credit spillover** is the understudied second-order risk. Regional banks’ exposures to nonbanks/private credit can become loss channels through warehouse lines, fund finance, and indirect lending if underwriting weakens or the private-credit market experiences redemption pressure. IMF’s April 2026 GFSR said financial stability risks remain elevated because of leverage across NBFIs, rising bank–NBFI interconnectedness, and vulnerabilities in private credit. Source set: Reuters Oct. 22, 2025; Reuters May 29 and June 5, 2026; IMF GFSR April 2026; verified 2026-07-28.
- Private credit has grown rapidly because post-2008 bank regulation and tighter capital rules pushed lending out of banks, while low rates, PE-backed borrower demand, and investor search for yield pulled capital into direct lending and asset-backed finance. Reuters reported U.S. banks’ loans to private credit providers at nearly $300B and loans to NDFIs at 10.4% of total bank loans vs. 3.6% a decade earlier; Reuters also reported private credit new loan issuance fell to $44.76B in the three months ended May 2026, about 40% below the prior quarter, indicating growth is now facing digestion rather than a clean straight line. Sources: Reuters Oct. 22, 2025; Reuters June 5, 2026; verified 2026-07-28.
- **EM local-currency debt** is a selective, not broad, opportunity: stronger EM policy frameworks and positive real yields help, but the trade remains sensitive to USD, US rates, and idiosyncratic fiscal risk. Source set: IMF April 2026; JPMorgan AM Q2 2026 outlook.
